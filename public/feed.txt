Street dogs and access to care

Overview

Updated November 11, 2026

A problem whose scale is unknown

Dogs are being abandoned and found roaming Stockton's streets. Some are lost pets, some have been dumped, and others live with people experiencing homelessness. Their circumstances and needs differ.

Stockton Animal Services recorded 3,106 stray-dog intake events in 2024. In September 2026, Stockton Street Dogs reported encountering three to ten dumped dogs per week. These figures establish that dogs are entering the care system and being encountered by rescuers, but they do not tell us how many dogs currently need help or how many are going without care.

The scale of the unmet need remains unknown.

Getting care isn't always straightforward

Stockton Animal Services has reported that its shelter is regularly at capacity. Healthy stray intake generally requires an appointment, while sick, injured, and aggressive dogs receive priority. Veterinary costs and the availability of rescue volunteers and foster homes can also affect the options for care. We do not yet know how often a request for help meets these limits or what happens afterward.

Help is spread across multiple organizations

Stockton Animal Services handles municipal sheltering and animal-control calls. Stockton Street Dogs works with dogs in the field through outreach, supplies, and placement efforts. Hard Luck offers subsidized veterinary treatment, and St. Mary's Pathways provides transitional housing that accommodates pets. These are different kinds of help; none of these roles guarantees that a particular dog can be taken in or treated when help is requested.

Capacity is expanding, but will it be enough?

Stockton is developing a municipal shelter expansion costing roughly $10.47 million and has authorized additional veterinary staffing and spay and neuter services. Separately, Stockton Street Dogs has proposed a Canine Campus for overflow and recovery; it is not operating. How much additional care these plans will deliver, and whether that will meet the need, remains unknown.

Our work

Reviewing the field-call records

Crossover received 7,183 Stockton Animal Services field-call records on November 2 and began reviewing them November 4. We are checking what each record says about a request, the response, and any recorded outcome, along with missing information and limits in the data. These calls cannot be counted as unique dogs or as the street population; findings from the review remain pending.

Getting closer to the problem

Crossover visited Stockton Animal Services, applied to volunteer there, asked about Doggie Day Out, and applied to volunteer with Stockton Street Dogs. These steps are ways to learn how care works in practice. The applications do not establish that we have participated or completed onboarding.

What's next

What can the records tell us?

First establish the calls' date range, categories, locations where recorded, and missing fields. Then examine recorded requests, response paths, and outcomes without treating a call as a dog or inferring an outcome the records do not contain.

How does a dog actually get help?

Follow up on the volunteer and Doggie Day Out inquiries, and ask care providers to describe the path from finding or reporting a dog to a response and possible care. Document what they confirm, where the path depends on capacity or eligibility, and where the answer remains unclear.

Open questions

How many dogs currently need help on Stockton's streets?

No verified count exists. We need to distinguish dogs who are lost, abandoned, roaming, or living with people, and learn what help each actually needs. A dog seen on the street should not automatically be counted as abandoned or in need of removal.

What happens when someone reports a dog?

We want to trace the report, any recorded response or disposition, and whether the dog actually reached care. The field-call records may describe only part of that path; an administrative outcome does not by itself establish the dog's later condition.

Where does access to care break down?

Which services can accept a dog at the time someone asks? Capacity, cost, eligibility, and transportation may affect the answer, but we have not established how often each is a barrier in Stockton. Provider follow-up and the records review may clarify this.
