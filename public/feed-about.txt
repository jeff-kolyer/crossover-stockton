Our mission

From work to vocation

AI and robotics are changing the relationship between work and survival. More of what people do today for wages may eventually be automated, accelerated, or made unnecessary. That raises a practical question: what should we do with greater freedom, capability, and time?

Crossover begins with one answer: turn toward real needs.

Instead of treating technology as an end in itself, Crossover explores how it can support a transition from compulsory work toward meaningful vocation: work chosen in response to reality, suffering, and unmet need.

This is an attempt to connect tools, attention, and action to the world as it is. The goal is to begin practicing a better future, not merely imagining one.

See clearly. Act locally.

Crossover Stockton is a public civic project based in Stockton, California. It starts with a gap: a need that is real, visible, and not being adequately met.

Stockton is where the founder has lived for more than a decade. It is a place where the project can meet people, visit institutions, and follow a need beyond what a website alone can show.

From there, we show up, look closely, learn from people involved, examine evidence, and take useful action. Understanding develops through observation, records, conversations, questions, and attempts to help.

The living feed is the public account of that work. Overview explains what is happening. Our work records what we have done and learned. What's next describes planned follow-up. Questions keeps uncertainty visible.

The collections in the left navigation—actions, activity, places, organizations, measurements, findings, and sources—hold the records and evidence behind the account.

We are trying to become informed in public. Before we can improve a situation, we have to understand it.

The work discovers the work.

Crossover does not begin with a master plan. It begins with attention to a specific need.

A record request may reveal a bottleneck. A field visit may expose a mistaken assumption. A conversation may uncover an existing effort. A small practical step may show what is possible, or what is missing.

The work discovers the work. We need enough contact with reality to take the next honest step, then let new evidence revise our understanding. A question may matter more than a conclusion, and the useful action may differ from the one we first imagined.

We make the process public: what we observed, what we found, what we tried, what remains unclear, and what might help next. The account can change as we learn.

This is a disciplined attempt to learn in public and act where action may be useful.
