A closer look at street dogs and access to care in Stockton.

We're examining the circumstances of dogs living or roaming on Stockton's streets, the organizations providing care, and what happens when someone seeks help. Through public records, firsthand visits, and conversations with care providers, we're working to understand the scale of unmet need, identify barriers to care, and find practical ways to improve the response.

The scale remains unknown

Dogs are being abandoned and found roaming Stockton's streets, but the scale of the problem remains unknown. Stockton Animal Services recorded 3,106 stray-dog intake events in 2024, while Stockton Street Dogs reported encountering 3–10 dumped dogs per week in September 2026. No verified count exists of dogs currently living or roaming on the streets.

Care exists, but access is limited

Stockton has an established animal-care network, but access to care is limited. The municipal shelter regularly faces capacity pressure, prioritizing emergencies while requiring appointments for healthy strays. Affordable veterinary care is in high demand, and rescue organizations depend on donations, volunteers, and available foster homes. Not every roaming dog is abandoned; some are lost or owned, including dogs living with people experiencing homelessness.

A network of care providers

Several organizations help fill these needs. Stockton Animal Services handles municipal sheltering and animal-control calls; Stockton Street Dogs provides outreach, supplies, and rescue assistance; Hard Luck offers subsidized veterinary treatment; and St. Mary's Pathways provides transitional housing that accommodates pets. Each service has limits, and actual availability is not always clear.

Plans to expand care

Efforts to expand care are underway. Stockton is developing a roughly $10.47 million animal shelter expansion and has authorized additional veterinary staffing and spay/neuter services. Stockton Street Dogs is proposing a Canine Campus for overflow and recovery. How much additional care these efforts will deliver remains to be established.

Reviewing the field-call records

Crossover is examining what happens when someone tries to help. We received 7,183 Animal Services field-call records on November 2 and are reviewing them alongside firsthand visits and provider outreach. The goal is to establish how many dogs need help, what happens when care is requested, where access breaks down, and what could improve the response.

What we’re trying to find out

  • How many dogs are currently on Stockton's streets, and what are their circumstances?
  • What happens when someone reports a stray, injured, or abandoned dog?
  • How many reported dogs ultimately receive care?
